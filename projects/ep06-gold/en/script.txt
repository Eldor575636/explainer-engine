## Why gold is valuable [CORE]
Let's start with the simplest question: why is gold expensive at all?
The first reason is that there is very little of it.
All the gold humans have ever mined, in the whole of history, comes to about two hundred and twenty thousand tonnes.
If you melted all of it into one block, you would get a cube about twenty-two metres on each side.
That is roughly the height of a seven-storey building.
Shared equally between every person on the planet, it would give each of us about twenty-seven grams.
At today's price, all of that gold is worth roughly twenty-seven trillion dollars, close to what the American economy produces in a year.
The second reason is that gold does not rust or rot.
Gold objects made thousands of years ago still shine as if they were new.
The third reason is that nobody can print it.
A central bank can create new money in a single day, but gold has to be dug out of the ground.
All the mines in the world produce about three thousand seven hundred tonnes a year, which adds only about two percent to the gold that already exists.
That is why people have trusted gold for thousands of years.
Around the sixth century before Christ, the kingdom of Lydia struck some of the world's first coins from a natural mix of gold and silver.

## Gold and money [CORE]
Because of that trust, money itself was tied to gold for a long time.
This was called the gold standard: you could take a paper note to the bank and exchange it for a fixed amount of gold.
After the Second World War, the world's currencies were tied to the dollar, and the dollar was tied to gold.
One ounce of gold was fixed at thirty-five dollars.
Compared with today's price, gold has become more than a hundred and ten times more expensive since then.
But in 1971, President Richard Nixon stopped exchanging dollars for gold.
Since then, our money has not been tied to any metal; its value rests only on trust.

## Britain's famous sale [LOCAL en]
In the decades that followed, many countries started to see gold as a relic of the past.
Britain went further than most.
Between 1999 and 2002, the UK Treasury sold three hundred and ninety-five tonnes of gold, about half of everything it owned.
The sales were announced in advance, and the price was already near a twenty-year low.
On average, Britain sold at about two hundred and seventy-five dollars an ounce.
That gold brought in roughly three and a half billion dollars.
Today the same gold would be worth about fifty billion dollars, fourteen times more.
Britain now holds only three hundred and ten tonnes, about four and a half grams for every person in the country.
Among economists, this sale became a famous example of selling at the wrong moment.

## Why they are buying again [CORE]
So if gold is a relic of the past, why are central banks buying it again?
In 2022, after Russia invaded Ukraine, Western countries froze about three hundred billion dollars of the Russian central bank's reserves held abroad.
After that, many countries realised something important: dollars or euros held in a foreign bank depend on another country's decisions.
Gold sitting in your own vault cannot be frozen with a single order.
After that, central banks bought more than a thousand tonnes of gold a year, three years in a row.
That is more than double the yearly average of the decade before.
In 2025, buying slowed a little, to eight hundred and sixty-three tonnes.
The biggest buyer for two years in a row was Poland, which bought one hundred and two tonnes in 2025 alone.
China, Kazakhstan, Turkey and Brazil were also near the top of the list.
Some countries started bringing their gold home.
Between 2013 and 2017, Germany moved six hundred and seventy-four tonnes of its gold from New York and Paris to Frankfurt.
Today Germany holds three thousand three hundred and fifty tonnes, the second-largest gold reserve in the world.
The result of all this buying is a big shift.
According to the European Central Bank, at the end of 2025 gold made up twenty-seven percent of the world's central bank reserves.
That is more than US Treasury bonds at twenty-two percent and the euro at fifteen percent.
In other words, gold has become the single largest asset in central bank vaults.

## America's gold [LOCAL en]
The largest gold reserve of all belongs to the United States: eight thousand one hundred and thirty-three tonnes.
About half of it sits in one building, the Bullion Depository at Fort Knox in Kentucky.
Fort Knox holds about one hundred and forty-seven million ounces, more than four and a half thousand tonnes.
At today's price, America's gold is worth roughly one trillion dollars.
But on the government's books it is still valued at a price set by law in 1973, forty-two dollars and twenty-two cents an ounce.
At that price, the same gold is worth only about eleven billion dollars.
Shared out among every American, the country's gold comes to about twenty-four grams per person, more than five times the British figure.
Across the Atlantic, the Bank of England's vaults in London hold more than four hundred thousand gold bars.
Each bar weighs about four hundred ounces and is worth around one and a half million dollars.
But most of that gold does not belong to Britain at all; the Bank looks after it for other countries' central banks.

## Gold and ordinary people [CORE]
Gold is not only for central banks.
In 2024, analysts at Wells Fargo estimated that Costco was selling between one hundred and two hundred million dollars of gold bars every month to its members.
Each of those bars weighs one ounce, and members were limited to a few bars at a time.
But there are three things worth knowing about gold.
First, gold pays no interest.
A savings account pays interest every year; a gold bar earns money only if its price goes up.
Second, the price of gold can swing sharply.
Since January this year, the price has fallen by almost thirty percent.
History has seen even longer slumps: in January 1980, one ounce of gold reached eight hundred and fifty dollars.
After that, it took twenty-eight years for the price to get back to that level.
Third, there is a gap between the buying price and the selling price.
On small bars and coins, that gap can be several percent, so if you sold on the same day you bought, you would lose part of your money.
That is why gold is used less for quick profit and more as long-term protection.

## The debate [CORE]
Among economists, there is a lively debate about gold.
One side says gold is the most reliable store of wealth in a crisis: when there is war, sanctions or high inflation, people run to gold first.
The other side says gold produces nothing.
The famous investor Warren Buffett made an interesting calculation in his 2011 letter to shareholders.
At that time, all the gold in the world was worth about nine point six trillion dollars.
By Buffett's estimate, that money could buy all the farmland in the United States and sixteen companies the size of Exxon Mobil, one of the most profitable companies in the world, with about a trillion dollars left over.
In his view, land and companies produce new income every year, while gold just sits in a vault.
Both sides have a point.
Gold is less a way to grow wealth and more a way to keep it.

## Why it matters to you [LOCAL en]
So why does this matter to you?
First, the price of gold is a kind of fear gauge: when it jumps, it usually means investors and governments are worried about wars, debt or inflation.
Second, central banks buying gold instead of US Treasury bonds is a slow vote of less confidence in the dollar, the currency your pay, your rent and your savings are measured in.
Third, if you see gold advertised as a safe place for your savings, remember that its price can fall as well as rise.
This is not financial advice, just the numbers worth knowing before any decision about your savings.

## The answer [CORE]
So, back to our question: why are central banks buying gold again?
Because the world is becoming less stable, and gold is the one form of wealth that is nobody else's debt.
It cannot be frozen, it cannot be printed, and it has kept its value for thousands of years.
But gold is not free of risk: its price goes up and down, sometimes for decades.
So it makes more sense to see gold not as a quick way to get rich, but as a way to protect wealth over the long run.

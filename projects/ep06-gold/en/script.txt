## Why gold is valuable [CORE]
First, a simple question: why is gold so expensive?
Reason one: there is very little of it.
Take a guess: if you melted down all the gold ever mined in human history, how big would the block be?
A football stadium?
A skyscraper?
The answer is a cube about twenty-two meters on each side.
That's roughly a seven-story building.
That's it, all of it, from the pharaohs until today: about two hundred and twenty thousand tonnes.
Split equally between everyone on the planet, you would get about twenty-seven grams, less than a single small bar.
And yet that one cube is worth about twenty-seven trillion dollars, close to what the entire American economy produces in a year.
Reason two: gold never rusts and never rots.
Gold jewelry buried for thousands of years comes out of the ground shining like new.
Reason three, and this is the big one: nobody can print it.
A central bank can create new money with a few keystrokes.
Gold has to be dug out of the ground, and all the mines in the world add only about two percent a year to what already exists.
That's why people have trusted gold for more than two and a half thousand years, ever since the kingdom of Lydia struck some of the world's first coins from a natural mix of gold and silver.

## Gold and money [CORE]
That trust was so strong that, for a long time, money itself was tied to gold.
Under the gold standard, you could walk into a bank with a paper note and swap it for a fixed amount of gold.
After the Second World War, the world's currencies were tied to the dollar, and the dollar was tied to gold, at thirty-five dollars an ounce.
Today's price is more than a hundred and ten times higher.
Then, in 1971, President Richard Nixon went on television and ended it.
Dollars could no longer be swapped for gold.
From that day on, the money in your wallet has not been backed by any metal at all; its value rests only on trust.

## Britain's famous sale [LOCAL en]
And once money no longer needed gold, many governments started to see gold as a relic.
None more than Britain.
Between 1999 and 2002, the UK Treasury sold three hundred and ninety-five tonnes of gold, about half of everything the country owned.
And here's the problem: it announced the sales in advance.
Traders knew a huge seller was coming, and the price, already near a twenty-year low, dropped even further.
Britain sold at an average of about two hundred and seventy-five dollars an ounce, and raised roughly three and a half billion dollars.
Today, that same gold would be worth about fifty billion dollars.
Fourteen times more.
Britain is now left with three hundred and ten tonnes, about four and a half grams for every person in the country.
Economists still use this sale as the classic example of selling at exactly the wrong moment.

## Why they are buying again [CORE]
So if gold is a relic, why are central banks suddenly buying it again?
The turning point came in 2022.
After Russia invaded Ukraine, Western countries froze about three hundred billion dollars of the Russian central bank's reserves held abroad.
Overnight, every government in the world learned a lesson.
Dollars or euros kept in someone else's bank are only yours as long as that other country agrees.
Gold in your own vault cannot be frozen with a single order.
What happened next was a buying spree.
Central banks bought more than a thousand tonnes a year, three years in a row, more than double the average of the decade before.
Even in 2025, a slower year, they bought eight hundred and sixty-three tonnes.
The biggest buyer two years running wasn't China or Russia.
It was Poland, with one hundred and two tonnes in 2025 alone.
Some countries didn't just buy more gold; they went and got back the gold they already had.
Between 2013 and 2017, Germany quietly moved six hundred and seventy-four tonnes of its gold from New York and Paris back home to Frankfurt.
And now comes the number that shows how big this shift really is.
According to the European Central Bank, at the end of 2025 gold made up twenty-seven percent of all central bank reserves in the world.
US Treasury bonds made up twenty-two percent, and the euro fifteen.
Gold is now the single biggest asset in the world's central bank vaults.

## America's gold [LOCAL en]
But the largest pile of gold on Earth doesn't belong to Poland or Germany.
It belongs to the United States: eight thousand one hundred and thirty-three tonnes.
About half of it sits in one building, the Bullion Depository at Fort Knox, Kentucky.
No visitors are allowed inside; over the years, only a few groups of officials and journalists have ever been let in.
At today's price, America's gold is worth roughly one trillion dollars.
But here's the strange part.
On the government's books, it is still valued at a price fixed by law in 1973: forty-two dollars and twenty-two cents an ounce.
At that price, the whole trillion-dollar pile is worth just eleven billion dollars on paper.
Shared out among every American, the country's gold comes to about twenty-four grams per person, more than five times the British figure.
And under the streets of London, the Bank of England guards more than four hundred thousand gold bars, each worth around one and a half million dollars.
Most of them don't even belong to Britain; the Bank keeps them for other countries' central banks.

## Gold and ordinary people [CORE]
But gold isn't only for governments.
In 2024, analysts at Wells Fargo estimated that Costco was selling between one hundred and two hundred million dollars of gold bars every month, right next to the bulk toilet paper.
So before anyone treats gold as a safe bet, here are three facts that rarely make it into the ads.
Fact one: gold pays nothing.
Money in a savings account earns interest every year; a gold bar earns you money only if its price goes up.
Fact two: the price can fall hard, and stay down.
In January 1980, gold hit eight hundred and fifty dollars an ounce.
It then took twenty-eight years to get back to that price.
Someone who bought at the top waited until 2008 just to break even.
Fact three: there is a gap between the price you buy at and the price you can sell at.
On small bars and coins, that gap can be several percent, so if you bought and sold on the same day, you would walk away with less.
That's why gold works less as a way to get rich quickly and more as long-term protection.

## The debate [CORE]
And that is exactly where economists split into two camps.
Camp one says gold is the most reliable store of wealth there is: in war, sanctions or high inflation, it's the first thing people run to.
Camp two says gold is a rock that just sits there.
Its most famous member is Warren Buffett.
In his 2011 letter to shareholders, he did a calculation.
Back then, all the gold in the world was worth about nine point six trillion dollars.
With that money, Buffett said, you could buy all the farmland in the United States, plus sixteen companies the size of Exxon Mobil, one of the most profitable companies on the planet.
And you would still have about a trillion dollars left over.
The farms would grow crops every year, the companies would pay profits every year, and the gold would do nothing at all.
So who's right?
In a way, both.
Gold isn't a machine for growing wealth; it's a safe for keeping it.

## Why it matters to you [LOCAL en]
So what does all this mean for you?
First, watch the gold price like a fear gauge: when it jumps, governments and investors are usually worried about wars, debt or inflation.
Second, every tonne central banks buy instead of US Treasury bonds is a quiet vote of slightly less confidence in the dollar, the currency your pay, your rent and your savings are measured in.
Third, the next time you see gold advertised as a safe place for your savings, remember 1980, and remember this year's thirty percent drop.
This isn't financial advice, just the numbers worth knowing before you decide anything.

## The answer [CORE]
So, back to where we started: why are central banks buying gold, even while its price is falling?
Because the world feels less safe, and gold is the only form of wealth that is nobody else's promise.
It can't be frozen, it can't be printed, and it has held its value for thousands of years.
But it isn't risk-free: its price rises and falls, sometimes for decades.
Gold won't make you rich quickly; what it does best is keep wealth safe for the long run.
